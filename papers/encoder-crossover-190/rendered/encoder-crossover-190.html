The small-encoder crossover is a prompted-baseline artefact: a fine-tuned encoder head loses to a fine-tuned 4B decision model by 0.33 and to a majority-class control

Aswin Ram Kalugasala Moorthy\\ Independent researcher\\ \texttt{aswinram.k.1603@gmail.com}Byline carried into this render from submission/main.tex.

Abstract

Every public "a small encoder wins" result we were able to locate inside the target corpus compares that encoder against a prompted (zero- or few-shot) LLM, never against a fine-tuned generative decider (the claim and its audit trail are internal to the corpus, not an external survey — §2). That a fine-tuned generative decider can beat a fine-tuned encoder at comparable effort is already public — a 2025 practitioner blog post reports exactly that on a binary ADE task (§2.1) — so we claim no priority for the direction; what we claim is this fixture, its controls and its diagnosis. We measure the fine-tuned comparison on a sealed typed-single-label decision fixture.

Headline. The best fine-tuned encoder head scores 0.3474 and loses to the fine-tuned 4B decider at 0.6789 — the declared primary baseline (author decision 2026-10-15), the auditable same-run arm — with paired McNemar b = 27, c = 90, ψ = 0.616, Δ = −0.3316, exact p = 4.167e−09. The control that could have contradicted this is the mandatory majority-class control, and it did contradict the candidate's framing of itself: majority-class returns 0.4526, beating every fine-tuned encoder head by +0.1053. The second control that could have contradicted the measurement is the shuffled-label arm, and it moved the other way from a spurious win: it returns 0.2316, below the fixture's 0.2911 chance floor. A third control guards instrument integrity: our n-gram leak criterion (≥ 2 distinct shared 8-grams within one row pair, both directions) flagged 0 of 152,000 training↔fixture pairs — and, to show the criterion can fire, it flagged three deliberately seeded copies at 13 / 44 / 57 shared 8-grams (pair maxima). Against the published, pre-registered baseline 0.6684 — which cannot be re-scored from the tree — the same head loses by 0.3210 ("0.32") against the secondary baseline 0.6684. The two figures use two baselines and must not be read as one: the headline 0.3316 ("0.33") is computed against the declared primary 0.6789, while 0.3210 ("0.32") is computed against the secondary/published 0.6684, which is the pre-registered F1 threshold and which cannot be re-scored from the tree.

Correction carried. Fixing three defects lowered the SetFit-MPNet arm monotonically (0.3158 → 0.2947 → 0.2632), but the same order-probe fix raised the ModernBERT-base arm (0.342105 → 0.347368). The universal claim "every correction moved the affected arm down" is false; the SetFit arm returned the opposite direction from the ModernBERT arm, which is the control on that claim.

Not in this paper. We make no latency claim: the encoder heads were timed locally in one forward batch while the 4B figures come from a served stack's stored rows, so the tree cannot support a fair latency comparison (see §6.1). We also do not report κ = 0.9014 (first blind reader) or κ = 0.8577 (second, independent blind reader) — nor their mutual κ = 0.9125 — as inter-annotator agreement: both labellers were models, so this is machine reproducibility of the fixture, not human agreement. The second reader partially replicates the first: the κ magnitude holds (both ≥ 0.85) but the exact 0.90 headline does not. An independent human labeller is still owed (§6.2).

1. Introduction

A common empirical shape in applied model-selection work is a crossover: at small label-set sizes N a large language model (LLM) wins or ties, and above some N a small fine-tuned encoder takes over. The canonical instance in the corpus we work from is an N-dependent table in which the encoder wins at N = 4 (AG News 94.11–94.63 vs 91.35; DBPedia at N = 14: 99.27–99.40 vs 98.83) and loses or ties at N = 2. The table's decisive property for this paper is not its numbers but its comparison class: the LLM side is an API LLM — i.e. prompted. We read that explicitly in the corpus's own errata (docs/DECIDER_STACK_DECISION.md §11.6 — source repository, not in the released package): "the N-dependent crossover table in §11.2 is encoder-versus-API LLM, i.e. prompted."

If the crossover is a property of prompting rather than of scale, then it should not survive when the generative side is given the same resource the encoder gets — supervised fine-tuning on the same task and the same labels. That is the hypothesis this paper tests, and the result is negative for the crossover.

Thesis. Every "a small encoder wins" result we were able to locate compares the encoder against a prompted LLM, never against a fine-tuned generative decider; under the fine-tuned comparison the crossover does not appear on our fixture.

Novelty, stated narrowly. We claim no priority for the direction of the result. That a fine-tuned generative model can beat a small fine-tuned encoder on a classification task is already in the public record: a 2025 practitioner experiment on binary adverse-drug-reaction classification reports a fine-tuned Llama-3.2-3B LoRA at F1 93.1 against ModernBERT-large at 89.2 at comparable training cost (Simmering, 2025 — a blog post, not peer-reviewed; §2.1). What is new here is narrower, and it is what we claim: (i) the sealed typed single-label decision fixture — 190 cases, N = 2–8 named options per case, 74 distinct labels — rather than binary sentence classification; (ii) the option-order permutation control, whose final pass flips 0 cases for every candidate head; and (iii) the majority-class control, which outranks every encoder head (0.4526 vs 0.3474, +0.1053) — a result the public counter-case does not contain. The contribution is the artefact diagnosis on this fixture, not the discovery that a fine-tuned generative decider can win.

Contributions.

  1. A like-for-like fine-tuned vs fine-tuned measurement on a sealed typed-single-label decision fixture: best fine-tuned encoder head 0.3474 against a fine-tuned 4B decider at 0.6789 (auditable, paired) and 0.6684 (published, not re-scorable). The direction is already public (§2.1); what is new is this fixture and its controls. §4.1–§4.2.
  2. The finding that the majority-class control beats every candidate head (0.4526 vs 0.3474, +0.1053) — so the interesting claim is not merely "the encoder loses to the 4B" but "the encoder loses to a constant answer". This control, the typed fixture and the order-permutation probe are the parts of the result for which we claim priority. §4.3.
  3. A calibration result whose verdict is convention-dependent: F3 fires on one stored ECE convention (head 0.0994 ≥ decider 0.0551) and does not fire on the other (0.0994 < 0.1547). We present both and name the ambiguity rather than resolving it. §4.6.
  4. A negative method result (M12), carried as a correction to the corpus's own summary: the claim that "every correction moved the affected arm down" is true for SetFit-MPNet only. §4.5.
  5. An explicit statement of what the evidence cannot support: latency (§6.1) and human agreement on the fixture labels — which two blind model passes do not supply, since the second only partially replicates the first (§6.2).

The paper is deliberately narrow. It is a single-fixture, single-host negative result, and §6 says so.

2. Related work

2.1 The works that make the "encoder wins" claim

The corpus's own audit of this literature lives in docs/DECIDER_STACK_DECISION.md §11.6, committed as ad301ae ("errata — the encoder recommendation was stronger than the evidence supports"). It is the citation for this paper's core literature claim, and it does support the claim — with an important qualification about its strength. Verbatim from §11.6:

"Every public 'a small encoder wins' result compares the encoder against a prompted, zero- or few-shot LLM — not against a fine-tuned generative decider. GLiNER's win is span extraction (exact-match F1), not a typed single-label decision; GLiClass has no measured LLM head-to-head at all (its own comparison table is qualitative); and the N-dependent crossover table in §11.2 is encoder-versus-API LLM, i.e. prompted. No published measurement was found of a small encoder beating a fine-tuned generative model on a typed single-label decision — verified-absent, and the second lane checked it explicitly."

And from the commit message of ad301ae:

"A second independent typed-channel lane ran with a stricter evidence base. Every public 'small encoder wins' result compares the encoder against a PROMPTED zero/few-shot LLM, not against a fine-tuned generative decider … Nobody has published the comparison we need, so our 190-case fixture remains the load-bearing evidence."

Qualification, stated rather than buried. This is a claim about a literature search, and the search protocol — databases, query terms, dates, inclusion rules — is not recorded in the corpus (this record's internal reference notes §B3; an internal working record not included in the released material). We therefore write "we were able to locate" and "no published measurement was found", not "no such result exists". A reviewer cannot audit a "verified-absent" claim whose search is unreported; this is the single largest sourcing gap in the paper (§6.4; recorded in this record's internal open-questions log, an internal working record not included in the released material).

The specific works, with the identifiers the corpus actually carries:

  • The N-dependent crossover table. arXiv 2602.06370, cited at DECIDER_STACK_DECISION.md §11.2. The corpus records no title, authors or venue; we cite the identifier only and flag the entry as incomplete in REFERENCES.bib. Its encoder-side wins, its latency spreads, and its cost figures are all recorded in the corpus, but its comparison class is a prompted API LLM.
  • A corroborating throughput result. arXiv 2512.12677, same section: ModernBERT-base classifies patents at 93.45 samples/s vs Llama-3.2-3B at 1.88 and Qwen3-4B at 1.26; distilling a fine-tuned 3B into ModernBERT-base is recorded as retaining ≥ 97 % of teacher F1 at ~45× throughput. Again: identifier only, no title/authors/venue in the corpus.
  • PriDe (option-ID token bias): arXiv 2309.03882, ICLR 2024, cited at DECIDER_STACK_DECISION.md:415 and :500. This grounds the position/order-bias concern that our own order-permutation probe measures (§3.4, §4.5).
  • Grammar-constrained decoding (GCD): arXiv 2305.13971, EMNLP 2023, and ACL 2025 Industry (2025.acl-industry.34), cited at DECIDER_STACK_DECISION.md:494–495. A3 does not use GCD; these are listed because the same errata section uses them, and they are not sources for the crossover claim.

The counter-case, now sourced — and what it does to our claim. The strongest single threat to a one-sided reading of our thesis is no longer unsourced, and we engage it rather than name it. It is a practitioner blog post, not a paper: Paul Simmering, "ModernBERT vs LLMs for Detecting Adverse Drug Reactions", published 12 January 2025, https://simmering.dev/blog/modernbert-vs-llm/ — no DOI, no venue, not peer-reviewed, cited as simmering2025modernbert. On binary adverse-drug-reaction sentence classification (ADE corpus, N = 2) it reports a fine-tuned Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct LoRA at F1 93.1 and 152 examples/s against ModernBERT-large at 89.2 and 87 examples/s, with equal fine-tuning effort per arm ("All setups can be trained for under one dollar and in less than 15 minutes"). We claim no priority for the direction of that result — a fine-tuned generative decider can beat a small fine-tuned encoder at comparable effort, and it is already public. Three properties separate its setting from ours: (i) its task is binary (N = 2) sentence classification, not a typed single-label decision over N = 2–8 named options; (ii) its decoder is a 3B model against a 395M encoder — a ≈7.6× scale confound the author names himself ("Did it win by sheer size?"); and (iii) its throughput edge is a serving-stack property: the author records that adding the 30 s vLLM CUDA-graph preparation time drops the LoRA arm to 42 examples/s, below ModernBERT-large, and warns that "this speed comparison was not comprehensive". Most importantly, the same experiment reproduces half of our diagnosis: its prompted, DSPy-optimised few-shot Llama-3.2-3B arm scored 80.7 F1, below ModernBERT-large's 89.2 — "Fine-tuning continues to outperform purely prompt-based approaches, even when those are optimized using automated prompt engineering." The counter-case is therefore not evidence against the prompted-baseline artefact: it is an independent, if non-peer-reviewed, reproduction of it on a different task, and it simultaneously sets the novelty boundary we accept above.

One corpus claim is now sourced, and we cite it for its result. The corpus's assertion that an encoder-only RoBERTa outperforms a fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B on 77-class intent classification is supported by Benayas, Sicilia & Mora-Cantallops (2024), A comparative analysis of encoder only and decoder only models in intent classification and sentiment analysis, Language Resources and Evaluation 59(3):2007–2030, DOI 10.1007/s10579-024-09796-y (benayas2024comparative). On BANKING77 (77 intents) the paper reports the encoder-only arm ahead of the fine-tuned decoder-only arm, and a decoder-only serving penalty of one to two orders of magnitude on a single T4 (29–108× at fixed output-token budgets). We cite it for that peer-reviewed, same-task encoder-over-decoder result; we do not reuse the corpus's "125M" formulation (the paper prints "approximately 110-120 million parameters") or its derived F1 gap, which the paper does not print. GLiNER and GLiClass, previously unsourced, now have identifiers and are treated in §2.2.

2.2 What is adjacent but not the same comparison

Four near-misses are worth separating from our claim, because each is a different task or a different comparison class; three of them are now sourced rather than merely named:

  • Span extraction vs typed single-label decision. GLiNER (arXiv 2311.08526; NAACL 2024, pp. 5364–5376, DOI 10.18653/v1/2024.naacl-long.300; zaratiana2024gliner) is a span-extraction (NER) model whose recorded win is exact-match F1 on spans, against zero-shot (prompted) LLMs — a span extractor and a typed decision head are not competing on the same object. One correction to the corpus's wording applies here: that characterisation is accurate for GLiNER 2023/2024 itself, but the corpus applies it to the model id fastino/GLiNER2.5-Decide, which is a text-classification decision model — GLiNER2 (arXiv 2507.18546; zaratiana2025gliner2) explicitly covers text classification, and the model card tags this variant a decision model for intent classification. We therefore cite GLiNER (2023/2024) for the span-extraction point and do not rest the claim on the decision-model variant. What GLiNER2's abstract does not state is whether its "LLM-based alternatives" comparison class is prompted or fine-tuned; we do not read it either way.
  • No measured LLM head-to-head. GLiClass (arXiv 2508.07662, 11 Aug 2025; stepanov2025gliclass) adapts the GLiNER architecture to sequence classification, and the corpus's claim that it has no measured LLM head-to-head at all is confirmed: its quantitative baseline tables contain only four cross-encoder/NLI models (deberta-v3-large-zeroshot-v2.0, deberta-v3-base-zeroshot-v2.0, roberta-large-zeroshot-v2.0-c, comprehend_it-base), no generative LLM (and no embedding/SetFit model) has any numeric row, GPT-4o appears only as a training-data annotator, and its LLM comparison is qualitative prose. This is an argument from absence, and we phrase it as one.
  • Prompted/typed deciders vs fine-tuned generative deciders. The corpus also carries a published laya-typed-decisions figure of 0.374 and an imajev-4b figure of 0.645. The laya figure is a prompted/typed baseline, not a fine-tuned decider. The imajev 0.645 is the H-A corpus stratum, not a fixture number — the fixture figure is 0.6368 (REPORT.md, §"Two things … NOT defects") — and we do not place 0.645 in any fixture table. Conflating the two would be a straight numerical error.
  • Single-label decision vs router/selection. A learned router deciding which model to call is a different object from a model deciding which label applies; we do not extend our result to routing.

Adjacent, citable neighbours — marked ADJACENT in REFERENCES.bib. Three further works were located while searching for external backing for §2.1's absence claim. They are real and traceable, and they are not sources for our central claim; none of them discharges §6.4's absence claim:

  • Kostina, Dikaiakos, Stefanidis & Pallis (2025), "Large Language Models For Text Classification: Case Study And Comprehensive Review", arXiv 2501.08457 (kostina2025llmtextclassification): an encoder-vs-LLM text-classification study whose LLM arms are prompted — it supports the prompted-baseline half, but it is not a survey establishing the absence of a fine-tuned-generative comparison.
  • Li, Sleem, Gentile, Nichil & State (2025), "Small Language Models in the Real World: Insights from Industrial Text Classification", ACL 2025 Industry Track, pp. 971–982, DOI 10.18653/v1/2025.acl-industry.68 (li2025smalllanguagemodels): the closest peer-reviewed equal-effort-adjacent evaluation found — it evaluates both prompt engineering and supervised fine-tuning for smaller models in industrial settings — but it is an evaluation, not a proof of absence, and its per-task results were not retrieved.
  • Weller, Ricci, Marone, Chaffin, Lawrie & Van Durme (2025), "Seq vs Seq: An Open Suite of Paired Encoders and Decoders", arXiv 2507.11412, accepted to ICLR'26 (weller2025seqvsseq): under "the same recipe for both encoder-only and decoder-only models" it reports that "encoder-only models excel at classification and retrieval tasks" and that "a 400M encoder outperforms a 1B decoder on MNLI" — matched-recipe, peer-reviewed support for the encoder side, but its headline task is MNLI (3-way), not a typed single-label decision over N = 2–8 named options.

Our claim is only about the comparison class: encoder-vs-prompted LLM does not license the inference encoder-vs-fine-tuned decider — and the one public fine-tuned-vs-fine-tuned counter-case (§2.1) is engaged, not ignored.

3. Method

3.1 The fixture

The instrument is a sealed set of 190 typed single-label decision cases drawn from a project's own decision corpus (evals/fixtures/reflex-decisions.ts in the source repository — not in the released package; the shipped copy is fixture/reflex-decisions.ts), with 721 options total, 74 distinct labels (51 of which occur exactly once), and per-case option counts distributed as K = 2 (33 cases), K = 3 (27), K = 4 (76), K = 5 (54). Its sha256 is 076164ac53c2c1aac130953345c481f88b51f4d6fb93d465c7430308a99b4796; the fixture was read once (fixtureReads = 1) and its digest was unchanged before and after the second-labeller pass, with the second labeller's sandbox copy byte-identical to the target.

Three instrument properties matter for reading the numbers, and one is routinely misread:

  • Chance floor = 0.2911. This is the mean of 1/K over the 190 cases, i.e. (33/2 + 27/3 + 76/4 + 54/5)/190 = 55.3/190 = 0.29105263…. It is not the majority-class prior. Do not conflate the 0.2911 floor with the 0.4526 majority-class control; they are different constructs computed from different things.
  • Option-0 rate = 0.3789; leaky majority rate over the fixture's own answers = 0.1474 (the rate at which the most common fixture answer, not option 0, is "always guess" — it is not the majority-class control either).
  • Label decidability is imperfect. The fixture was authored by one worker and carries known flagged weak points; see §6.2 and the five owed corrections listed in this record's internal checklist (an internal working record not included in the released material).

3.4 Controls — each one is asked what it would have shown

A control that cannot report the opposite is decoration. Three controls are load-bearing here, plus fixture-reliability and leak-detection probes:

  • Majority-class control (0.4526). Could have contradicted the candidate: if any head exceeded 0.4526, the "encoder head is a viable default" reading would survive on this fixture. It returned the opposite: no head does; the margin is +0.1053 over the best head.
  • Shuffled-label control (0.2316). Could have contradicted the measurement: if a head trained on shuffled labels scored at or above the 0.2911 chance floor, the fixture would be leaking label signal through some channel other than the intended task, and every accuracy number above the floor would be suspect. It returned the opposite: 0.2316 < 0.2911, below chance. Note the associated calibration caveat: its temperature-scaling fit hit the grid ceiling (100.0, onGridCeiling = true), so its reported ECE 0.0808 is an upper bound on the calibration reachable by that arm, not a fitted value.
  • F4 n-gram leak lint. Could have contradicted instrument integrity: rule = "drop a row when it shares ≥ 2 distinct 8-grams with any single row of the other side", applied in both directions, threshold 2. Over the real comparison it compared 152,000 training↔fixture pairs in each direction and flagged 0, removing 0 rows (and 160,000 training↔dev pairs, also 0). Could it ever fire? The positive controls: three deliberately seeded copies, which fired at 13 / 44 / 57 shared 8-grams. Read those as per-pair maxima: 13 is the state-only copy's total and its max-pair count; 44 and 57 are the max-pair counts of the boilerplate-only and whole-row copies, whose totals are 220 and 233. A novel row shared 0 grams with anything (negative control). So the lint fires when it should and is silent when it should be — F4 not firing means the run is not void.
  • Order-permutation probe. A PriDe-style option-order probe. In the buggy pass-1 it flipped answers on 138 (ModernBERT), 142, 101, 135 and 81 cases depending on arm; in the final pass it flips 0 for all candidate heads and 58 for the majority-class control. The control retaining flips is the right behaviour: a constant-answer arm is genuinely order-sensitive by construction, so a probe that reported 0 flips for it would be broken.
  • Two blind model readers — machine reproducibility only. Reader B (routeB-blind-model) reached agreement 0.9053, κ 0.9014, 18/190 disagreements; reader B2 (mimo-v2.6-flash) reached 0.8632, κ = 0.8577, 26/190 — a partial replication whose exact "0.90" headline degrades by ~4 points. The two readers agree with each other at 0.9158, κ = 0.9125. Both are models, not independent humans, so neither figure — and not the model-to-model κ either — is inter-annotator agreement (§6.2). The deterministic lexical route (routeA) is not a labeller at all: it is a rule, and its κ = 0.3249 is the floor a rule-only approach reaches.

4. Results

4.1 The five arms on the sealed fixture

Best fine-tuned encoder head: 0.3474 (modernbert-base; deberta-v3-base ties it at 0.3474 but did not train — §3.2). setfit-mpnet: 0.2632. Every one of these is barely above the fixture's 0.2911 chance floor, and setfit-mpnet is below it. The controls bracket the candidates: majority-class 0.4526 above, shuffled-label 0.2316 below.

The pre-registered falsifier F1 fired. It was written as "encoder accuracy < 0.6684 (the best 4B)" (preregistration/EXP12-typed-channel-comparison-prereg.md:100, REPORT.md:85); the best head is 0.3474, so F1 fires against the published figure — and, separately, 0.3474 < 0.6789, so it fires against the auditable one too.

A correction we must carry, not a claim we may repeat. The corpus's own summary (the paper-candidates record :269 and experiment-learnings record :281) states "Every correction moved the affected arm DOWN." That is false as a universal. The released results/REPORT.md carries the defect write-up (the standalone results/DEFECT-EVIDENCE.json is not part of the released set), and it shows:

  • modernbert-base moved UP: 0.342105 (pass 1, buggy order probe) → 0.347368 (final) once the order-probe bug was fixed.

So the correct statement is: "every correction moved the SetFit arm down", or equivalently "all three defects flattered the candidate" — the defects all favoured the candidate, but the fixes did not lower every arm. The SetFit arm is the control that returned the expected direction; the ModernBERT arm is the control that refuted the universal quantifier. We state the corrected version throughout and repeat it in §5.3. The baseline declaration is not implicated here; this correction stands regardless of baseline.

Related numbers in the same chain: SetFit's ECE moved 0.1665 → 0.2738 (worse as accuracy dropped), with the final 0.2632 arm at ECE 0.1536; its order-permutation flips moved 101 → 46 → 36 → 0, so the final arm is order-stable. Pass-1 flips for the other arms were 138 (ModernBERT), 142, 101, 135, 81; the final pass reports 0 for all candidate arms.

4.3 The control that beats the candidates

The majority-class control returns 0.4526 — a constant answer beats every fine-tuned encoder head on this fixture, by a margin of +0.1053 over the best one (0.45263157894736844 − 0.3473684210526316 = 0.10526315789473684). This is the result we consider hardest to explain away, and it is why the paper's claim is stronger than "the 4B wins": on this fixture, the encoders are not merely behind the fine-tuned decider, they are behind a fixed decision rule.

shuffled-label returns 0.2316, i.e. 0.23157894736842105 — below the 0.2911 chance floor, which is the direction that keeps the instrument credible (§3.4).

4.5 Power

The observed harm is 0.33157894736842103 / 0.15949356562876219 = **2.079×** the paired MDE, and 2.446× the unpaired MDE. The conclusion holds against either.

⚠ We do not reuse the phrase "paired is conservative." On this run it is false. ψ = 0.616 is high, so unpaired/paired = sqrt((p₁(1−p₁)+p₂(1−p₂))/ψ) = 0.85× — the paired MDE (0.1595) is the LARGER of the two, not the smaller. The requirement to quote the paired figure (method learning M11) still stands; the justification that it is the lenient or conservative choice does not. We say what the numbers are.

4.6 Calibration — and the convention that decides it

The best head's ECE is 0.0994 (0.09936282184403895). The decider-4b's stored ECE is 0.0551 under one convention and 0.1547 under the other. The pre-registered F3 falsifier therefore flips:

  • on probabilities[choice]: head 0.0994 ≥ decider 0.0551 → F3 fires;
  • on the stored confidence field: 0.0994 < 0.1547 → F3 does not fire.

Same rows, opposite verdicts. results/REPORT.md:11 records fires: ["F1", "F3"] and the state CLOSE: F1 and F3 fired; F2, F4, F5 and F6 did not. ⚠ We flag this ambiguity rather than resolving it. The paper's calibration claim is conditional on the convention, and the convention must be named wherever the claim appears. Temperature scaling was fitted on a geometric grid from 1e−3 to 1e2, 200 points; per-arm temperatures are 1.0970 (ModernBERT), 0.0036 (DeBERTa — did not train), 39.63 (SetFit), 1.0 (majority-class), 100.0 (shuffled-label, on grid ceiling).

  • H4 is not supported. The accuracy ordering is monotone in parameter count across the three heads (0.2632 at 109M < 0.3474 = 0.3474 at 149M and 184M), so there is no size-crossover story within these heads. results/REPORT.md:109 records this explicitly.
  • The H-A/H-B strata. The same decider-4b reproduction scores 0.6650 on the H-A corpus stratum (reproducing its published 0.665) and 0.67 on H-B. These are corpus strata, not fixture numbers, and we do not compare them to fixture accuracy. imajev-4b's 0.645 is likewise an H-A corpus stratum figure; its fixture figure is 0.6368.

5. Discussion

5.1 The crossover is a property of the baseline, not of the model class

The published crossover compares an encoder against a prompted LLM. When the generative side is fine-tuned on the same task with the same labels, the crossover does not appear on our fixture: the encoder is 0.3316 behind on the declared primary baseline (0.6789) and 0.3210 behind on the secondary, published one (0.6684). The effect size is not marginal under either pairing: Δ = −0.3316 exceeds 2× either MDE, with exact p = 4.167e−09, and the paired bootstrap CI [−0.4316, −0.2263] excludes zero.

The honest reading is narrower than "encoders are worse". Prompting and fine-tuning are different resources; the published results show what a prompted LLM does with a task description and a few examples, and this paper shows what happens when that constraint is removed on this one fixture. The crossover may well be a real property of the prompting regime. What we have shown is that it does not transfer for free to the fine-tuning regime — which is exactly the inference the corpus's own errata says the literature does not license.

We claim no priority for the direction. That a fine-tuned generative decider beats a fine-tuned encoder at comparable effort is already public (§2.1, Simmering 2025 — a blog post, not peer-reviewed). What this fixture contributes is the artefact diagnosis and, sharper than the direction, the majority-class control that beats every candidate head (§5.2) — neither of which the counter-case contains.

5.2 The majority-class control is the sharpest result

We expected the interesting comparison to be encoder vs fine-tuned 4B: 0.3474 vs 0.6789. The sharper finding is 0.3474 vs 0.4526: a constant answer beats every fine-tuned encoder head by +0.1053, and setfit-mpnet (0.2632) sits below the 0.2911 chance floor. This reframes the paper. It is not "the 4B is better than the encoder"; it is "on this fixture the encoder heads fail to beat a fixed rule, and the fine-tuned 4B is the only arm that is clearly doing the task". Any recommendation of the form "use an encoder head as the default at N ≥ 4" must clear the majority-class bar on the target fixture first — a bar that costs one re-score to compute and that the corpus's prior recommendation had not cleared.

5.3 Method lessons, including one correction to the record

  • A defect chain can favour the candidate without lowering every arm. We carry the corrected statement: the corrections lowered the SetFit arm (0.3158 → 0.2947 → 0.2632) and raised the ModernBERT arm (0.342105 → 0.347368). The universal form of the claim ("every correction moved the affected arm DOWN") is false and is not repeated anywhere in this paper.
  • The quoted MDE is the paired one; it is not the lenient one here. ψ = 0.616, unpaired/paired = 0.85×. Quote the paired figure because M11 requires it, not because it flatters the conclusion.
  • A calibration verdict can be decided by a storage convention. F3 fires on probabilities[choice] and not on confidence — same rows, opposite verdicts. Any calibration claim must name the field it reads.
  • A mandatory control is only meaningful if it can win — and here it did. The majority-class control beat every candidate. The design lesson is to pre-register the control's ability to contradict, not just its presence.

6. Limitations

6.1 Latency was out of scope, and the evidence could not support it

We make no latency claim, in the abstract, the results or anywhere else. The evidence tree cannot support a fair latency comparison, for four recorded reasons:

  1. Never co-measured. REPORT.md:191: "The heads were timed on the GX10's GB10 with local weights; the decider's 0.075 s/decision figure and the re-scored 0.0531 s/decision on these rows come from a served model on a different stack."
  2. Different unit of work. The head figure is one full per-decision call — all option texts tokenised, one forward batch, softmax (12.37 / 14.16 / 11.96 / 12.46 ms for the four heads; 0.003 ms for majority-class; n = 570 after 20 warm-up cases, GPU synchronised). The decider figure is a served autoregressive decode loop. Those are not the same operation.
  3. The F6 threshold is a constant, not a measurement. F6 compares heads against the pre-registered 0.075 s/decision (results/REPORT.md:107, which records the DECIDER_TIER §2 figure), so F6 not firing (fires = false) only says 12–14 ms < 75 ms. It is not evidence from a head-to-head.
  4. The decider's own timing spreads 2× across strata of the same model — 53.06 ms (instrument190), 72.85 ms (H-B), 106.92 ms (H-A) — which by itself shows the timing was not controlled. (imajev-4b and laya sit at 453 ms / 639 ms in the same stored source, but comparing them to the local head timings would repeat the same category error.)

The strongest defensible statement is the uninteresting one: per-decision, the heads sit in the tens of milliseconds and the stored decider figures sit in the tens-to-hundreds of milliseconds, measured on different stacks and different operations. That is not a result, so latency is dropped and named here instead. Closing it would need a 4B decider re-measured on the same host under the same per-decision protocol.

6.2 Two blind model readers: machine reproducibility of the fixture, not inter-annotator agreement

Two blind model passes now exist. Reader B (routeB-blind-model) agrees with the fixture's hand-authored labels 0.9053 of the time, κ = 0.9014, with 18/190 disagreements. Reader B2 (mimo-v2.6-flash), run later on the same blinded inputs, agrees 0.8632 of the time, κ = 0.8577, with 26/190 disagreements. The two readers agree with each other 0.9158 of the time, κ = 0.9125 — a genuinely new, model-to-model quantity. The magnitude of the first result therefore replicates (both readers land at κ ≥ 0.85), but the exact "0.90" headline does not: reader B2 sits 4.2 points lower on agreement and 4.4 points lower on κ, and the pre-registered ladder would place it in band 2 (0.75 ≤ agreement < 0.90) while it still clears the band-1 κ threshold. We report this as a partial replication and give both readers, not one of them as "the" figure.

The two readers agree with each other more than either agrees with the fixture record (0.9158 > 0.9053 and > 0.8632). That is consistent either with a shared LLM reading bias or with author-idiosyncratic conventions in the fixture, and we do not resolve it: a high model-to-model κ is equally consistent with two models sharing a wrong convention. What it does rule out is treating the fixture's conventions as self-evident, and it is why those conventions should be read as conventions rather than as ground truth.

Four further findings bound how the labels should be read:

  • The weak dimensions are the same for both readers, and the weakest is memory.admission (B2 κ = 0.5625, B κ = 0.7153), followed by acquisition (B2 0.7045, B 0.8049). The other five of the seven kinds (escalation, topology, worker.class, context.package, capability.family) are stronger for both readers. Consistent weakness across two independent machine readers points at the fixture, not at the readers: label soundness is dimension-dependent, and the dependence looks like a property of the instrument.
  • The memory.admission tier boundary is a real, replicated ambiguity — the top repair item. Four cases (memory.admission-001/-009/-021/-027) record step-scoped scratch that is never re-read as EPHEMERAL; both blind readers independently chose DROP on all four (4/4 replicated). B first reported this convention and B2 shows it is not one model's quirk. The fixture's tier vocabulary for scratch that is never re-read is not pinned down by the questions.
  • Accuracy tracks option arity for both readers — but the effect is confounded with kind. Agreement falls from 0.970 (2-option cases, n = 33) to 0.722 (5-option cases, n = 54) for B2, and from 1.000 to 0.815 for B (κ 0.950 → 0.684 and 1.000 → 0.791). The 5-option stratum is dominated by the closed-roster kinds memory.admission and acquisition — the two hardest kinds for independent semantic reasons — so this is not clean evidence of option-wording exploitation, and on the stratum where the answer is named in the question (30 escalation cases) the readers scored 30/30 (B) and 28/30 (B2), which is not how a surface-cue matcher behaves. But the gradient is real and it weakens any blanket "the labels are sound" claim for high-arity closed-roster kinds: soundness is measurably lower there.
  • Disagreement structure, not just its count. B2 disagrees on 26 cases and B on 18, but the overlap is a 14-case shared contested core — all four acquisition traps (-007/-014/-019/-023), topology-018, capability.family-004/009, context.package-004, worker.class-023, the four EPHEMERAL/DROP cases above, and memory.admission-018 (DROP→EPISODIC). The tails (12 B2-only, 4 B-only) are scattered and read as reader-specific noise, not as a second catalogue of defects. The honest reading is "14 shared contested cases plus two small reader-specific tails", not "26 defects".

Two further honest caveats:

  • The pooled κ is weak on its own: 51 of 74 labels occur once, so pe is tiny (0.040) and κ is dominated by po. The informative figures are the stratified ones: closed-vocabulary subset (4 kinds, 106 cases) κ = 0.8837; roster-and-bespoke subset (84 cases) κ = 0.9140; AB mutual agreement 0.3684; the deterministic lexical rule (routeA — a rule, not a labeller) reaches only κ = 0.3249 (agreement 0.3421), and in 4 of the 18 disagreements routeA backs the fixture record. Route A is a tokeniser-overlap rule rather than a model, so its κ moves by a few thousandths with how the fixture's text is spelled and cased — the measured range across our neutralisation rounds was 0.319886… to 0.324881… — an order of magnitude smaller than its ≈0.33 separation from the decider, so no conclusion rests on the exact value.
  • A threshold was pre-registered (≥ 0.90 agreement / ≥ 0.85 κ). Reader B met it at 0.905 / 0.901; reader B2 met the κ threshold but not the agreement threshold, at 0.863 / 0.858. But a met threshold on a model pass — or on two — does not convert it into human agreement.

An independent human labeller is still owed, and the paper-candidates record §F.3 is not discharged. the second-labeller-190 source experiment tree's evidence record :309 states it for one pass — "It is not IAA. One pass is a model." — and two passes do not change that: κ = 0.9125 between two models is still not inter-annotator agreement and must never be cited as such. Two models can share a wrong convention as easily as a right one, and this result bounds machine reproducibility for exactly two models on exactly one fixture. §F.3's point — that several candidate papers share this one 190-case fixture, so "one fixture flaw propagates into four papers" — remains true, and its call for a second independent human labeller is not discharged by either pass. The fixture's own flagged decidability weak points (five owed corrections, of which four are repairs to the fixture file itself) are listed in this record's internal checklist (an internal working record not included in the released material) and were deliberately not applied, because the target repository is strictly read-only.

6.3 External validity: one fixture, one host, and a baseline choice awaiting a human

This is a single 190-case fixture authored by one worker, evaluated on one host. If a reviewer judges the fixture unrepresentative, the claim reduces to "on these 190 cases" — still a publishable negative result, but a smaller one. The fixture shares its provenance with several other candidate papers (§F.3). No external typed-decision benchmark was used, and none was found in the corpus to use.

The primary baseline is declared: 0.6789 (author decision, 2026-10-15) — the auditable same-run figure. 0.6684 is carried as a secondary / robustness figure and as the pre-registered F1 threshold, and it cannot be re-scored. The seven sentences that would change if that declaration were ever revisited are enumerated in §3.3; the conclusion does not depend on the choice.

6.4 The literature claim is an internal verified-absent audit; its counter-case is a sourced blog post

§2's core claim is supported by docs/DECIDER_STACK_DECISION.md §11.6 and commit ad301ae, which state it as an errata on the corpus's own earlier recommendation. It does support the claim as written — but it is a claim about a literature search whose protocol is not recorded: databases, query terms, dates and inclusion rules are absent from the corpus. We write "we were able to locate" rather than "no such work exists", and our external search then failed to upgrade the hedge: no survey or recorded search protocol establishing the absence was located, so the weaker phrasing stays.

The corpus's own equal-fine-tuning-effort counter-case, previously without an identifier, is now located and cited — as a blog post, deliberately not dressed as a paper (§2.1, simmering2025modernbert). It reports a fine-tuned Llama-3.2-3B LoRA at F1 93.1 beating ModernBERT-large's 89.2 on binary ADE classification at equal per-arm training cost, and we claim no priority for that direction. Our thesis is therefore supported by one fine-tuned-vs-fine-tuned data point against the crossover on our own fixture, plus that post's independent reproduction of the prompted-baseline half (its prompted arm scored 80.7) — not by a survey. The corpus claim that was carried without an identifier — an encoder-only RoBERTa beating a fine-tuned LLaMA2-7B on 77-class intent classification — is now sourced to Benayas, Sicilia & Mora-Cantallops (2024), Language Resources and Evaluation 59(3):2007–2030, DOI 10.1007/s10579-024-09796-y, and cited for that result.

6.5 Scope of the candidate arms

Only three encoder families were run, all 109M–184M parameters: ModernBERT-base, DeBERTa-v3-base and SetFit-MPNet. deberta-v3-base did not train at the pre-registered operating point (train loss 1.1288 → 1.1235, results/REPORT.md:151; dev 0.50 → 0.495 against a dev majority-class baseline of 0.475), so it is not a completed arm and we do not rely on it. Training is not bit-reproducible — three identical runs gave ModernBERT epoch-1 dev accuracies 0.5150 and 0.5900 and fixture accuracies 0.3421 and 0.3474 — so a ±1–2 point band applies to every head number here. All conclusions have margins far larger than that band, but a reader should not treat 0.3474 as exact. A reviewer's obvious fourth arm — a trained linear probe on a frozen LLM embedding — was not run and is not present anywhere in the evidence tree; it is listed as an open decision in this record's internal open-questions log (an internal working record not included in the released material). H4 (accuracy monotone in parameter count) is not supported; the observed ordering is monotone, so there is no within-heads size-crossover story to tell.

6.6 The counter-case asks whether our encoder arm is simply under-trained

A reviewer who has read the equal-effort counter-case can put the objection in one sentence, and we write it here in their voice rather than waiting for it:

"Your best encoder head scores 0.3474 and fails to beat a majority-class control at 0.4526, while a real ModernBERT-large reached 0.892 F1 in the counter-case. Is your encoder arm under-trained — so that your shortfall measures your training recipe rather than the crossover you claim to be diagnosing?"

We cannot fully answer that objection from our evidence, and we do not pretend to. What we can say is bounded, item by item:

  • The 0.892 is a different instrument and is not comparable. The counter-case's 89.2 F1 is binary (N = 2) sentence classification on the ADE corpus; our fixture is a typed single-label decision over N = 2–8 named options, with 74 distinct labels (51 occurring exactly once) and a 0.2911 mean-1/K chance floor. Absolute accuracy or F1 across those two instruments is not a like-for-like comparison in either direction, and we do not use it as one.
  • What the majority-class control licenses — and what it does not. It licenses exactly one claim: on this fixture, no encoder head is a viable default — 0.4526 beats the best head by +0.1053, computed on the same 190 cases and the same option orders. It does not license the claim that modernbert-base is well tuned, that 0.3474 is an encoder ceiling, or that an encoder could not reach a higher absolute level on this fixture under a better recipe. We report the encoder arm's absolute level as an observed value under one recorded recipe, not as an architecture verdict.
  • What we do not establish about tuning effort. This paper reports no tuning-effort or compute study for the encoder arms. The counter-case's equal-effort premise (under one dollar, under 15 minutes per arm on one A10G) is its premise on its task; we did not reproduce it, and we make no equal-effort claim of our own. Training non-reproducibility (§6.5: three identical runs gave fixture accuracies 0.3421 and 0.3474, a ±1–2 point band) bounds how much a single head number can carry — but a ±1–2 point band is not evidence that the recipe is adequate, and we do not offer it as such.
  • The reviewer's alternative reading stays open. If the encoder arm is under-trained, part of the 0.3316 shortfall could reflect the recipe rather than the crossover. Nothing in this paper excludes that reading. It is named as an open question in this record's internal unsourced-claims log (an internal working record not included in the released material); closing it would need a tuning-effort sweep on this fixture — including the fourth arm named in §6.5, a trained linear probe on a frozen LLM embedding, which was not run — and we did not run one.
  • What the claim therefore is. A change of direction under fine-tuning on one sealed fixture, plus the prompted-baseline artefact diagnosis — not an estimate of an encoder ceiling, and not an equal-effort law. It does not depend on 0.3474 being the best an encoder can do; it depends on the paired fine-tuned comparison, the majority-class control, and the fact that the published encoder-wins results compare the encoder against a prompted LLM.

Honest limitation: the harness does not ship, so the measurement cannot be re-run. The six Python steps (prepare.py, train.py, evaluate.py, analyze.py, defect-probe.py, collect.py), the two TypeScript steps (export-fixture.ts, lint.ts) and the harness's frozen data/ are withheld. The subset ships their outputs — the sealed fixture and the per-arm and control records in results/ — and verify/recompute-claims.py recomputes every cited value from those records. What a reader cannot do is retrain an arm, re-render the model inputs, or re-run the evaluation pipeline. We do not claim that the measurement is re-runnable.

A provenance fact a reader should not have to guess. The EXP#12 harness is not on the source repository's default branch: the experiment closed that branch unmerged (its name and commit are withheld), at path tools/typed-channel/, and that path does not exist on trunk. The 18 harness files were recovered read-only from the git object store (git cat-file blob) and are not part of the released set; a reader should not assume they are on the default branch.

Withheld by policy — deliberately, not by oversight. The agent-state kernel / runtime implementation (the target repository's withheld implementation directories) is the system the measurement is about, and it is not released. No model weights are released: the three arms are fetched as public checkpoints at run time (answerdotai/ModernBERT-base, Apache-2.0; microsoft/deberta-v3-base, MIT; sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2, Apache-2.0), and no weight file is shipped or present in the source repository's tree. The source repository's 26-file evidence tree is likewise not released. The full boundary is enumerated in the subset's accompanying exclusion inventory — 15 exclusion classes, including traces, run artefacts, scratch trees and vendor code. Checking this paper's claims requires the fixture, the verification tooling and the result records; it does not require the kernel, the weights, or the training pipeline. Reproducing the measurement is not possible from the released material, because the harness is withheld.

Compute and resources

All encoder-arm measurements reported here were run on a single host (GX10 gx10-163b, NVIDIA GB10 GPU); the fine-tuned 4B decider figures are re-scored from stored rows produced by a served stack on different hardware and were not co-measured here (§6.1). Per-decision head timing is one batched forward call over all option texts, 20 warm-up cases and 3 × 190 = 570 timed cases with the GPU synchronised (§6.1). The three encoder arms are public Hugging Face checkpoints fetched at run time; no model weights ship (see Data and code availability). This paper reports no tuning-effort or compute study for the encoder arms (§6.6). The training harness is withheld from release and no training run was re-executed for this paper, so wall-clock training time per arm, GPU memory footprint, and any energy measurement are not recorded in the released record; those figures are stated here as unrecorded rather than filled with an estimate.
