## 2. Cap-exempt H-1B

### 2.1 A correction to this memo's own brief

The unit brief cited **8 CFR 214.2(h)(8)(ii)(B)** as the cap-exempt provision. **That citation is wrong on
the current text.** I opened the paragraph and it reads:

> "(B) When an approved petition is not used because the beneficiary(ies) does not apply for admission to
> the United States, the petitioner shall notify the Service Center Director who approved the petition that
> the number(s) has not been used…"
> — 8 CFR 214.2(h)(8)(ii)(B), read 2026-10-14

That is about **unused visa numbers**, not exemptions. **The operative cap-exemption paragraphs are
8 CFR 214.2(h)(8)(iii)(F)** (employer-based exemptions, cross-referencing INA 214(g)(5)(A)–(B)) and
**8 CFR 214.2(h)(19)(iii)(C)** (the definitions). Anyone repeating the (h)(8)(ii)(B) citation should stop.

### 2.2 THE STATUTE SAYS — INA 214(g)(5), 8 U.S.C. 1184(g)(5)

Source: govinfo, *United States Code, 2022 Edition*, Title 8, §1184 —
<https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2022-title8/html/USCODE-2022-title8-chapter12-subchapII-partII-sec1184.htm>
(read 2026-10-14). Verbatim subparagraphs:

> "(A) is employed (or has received an offer of employment) at an institution of higher education (as
> defined in section 1001(a) of title 20), or a related or affiliated nonprofit entity;
> (B) is employed (or has received an offer of employment) at a nonprofit research organization or a
> governmental research organization; or
> (C) has earned a master's or higher degree from a United States institution of higher education (as
> defined in section 1001(a) of title 20), until the number of aliens who are exempted from such numerical
> limitation during such year exceeds 20,000."

**Read this carefully — it is the single most useful distinction in the memo.** The four employer-type
exemptions are **(g)(5)(A) and (B)**. Subparagraph **(C) is not an employer exemption at all**: it is the
"US master's degree" exemption, it is still **capped at 20,000** and still runs through the lottery. A
person without a US master's cannot use (C). The exemption (C) confers is real but narrow — its own
**separate 20,000 pool**, still capped and lottery-subject — but it does **not** deliver the cap-exempt treatment (A)/(B) do, so it is not this profile's route. *(Loosened by UCORR2 per UVER2: "would not help them even if they had one" was too strong in general; the point is kept.)*

### 2.3 THE REGULATION SAYS — 8 CFR 214.2(h)(8)(iii)(F)

Source: eCFR Title 8 (via the Cornell LII e-CFR mirror, <https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/214.2>,
read 2026-10-14; eCFR itself states "Displaying title 8, up to date as of 10/13/2026"). Verbatim:

> "(F) Cap exemptions under sections 214(g)(5)(A) and (B) of the Act. An alien is not subject to the
> numerical limitations identified in section 214(g)(1)(A) of the Act if the alien qualifies for an
> exemption under section 214(g)(5) of the Act. For purposes of section 214(g)(5)(A) and (B) of the Act:
> (1) 'Institution of higher education' has the same definition as described at section 101(a) of the
> Higher Education Act of 1965 (20 U.S.C. 1001(a)).
> (2) A nonprofit entity shall be considered to be related to or affiliated with an institution of higher
> education if it satisfies any one of the following conditions: (i) The nonprofit entity is connected to or
> associated with an institution of higher education through shared ownership or control by the same board
> or federation; (ii) The nonprofit entity is operated by an institution of higher education; (iii) The
> nonprofit entity is attached to an institution of higher education as a member, branch, cooperative, or
> subsidiary; or (iv) The nonprofit entity has entered into a formal written affiliation agreement with an
> institution of higher education that establishes an active working relationship between the nonprofit
> entity and the institution of higher education for the purposes of research or education, and a
> fundamental activity of the nonprofit entity is to directly contribute to the research or education
> mission of the institution of higher education. A nonprofit entity may engage in more than one
> fundamental activity.
> (3) An entity is considered a 'nonprofit entity' if it meets the definition described at paragraph
> (h)(19)(iv) of this section. 'Nonprofit research organization' and 'governmental research organization'
> have the same definitions as described at paragraph (h)(19)(iii)(C) of this section.
> (4) An H-1B beneficiary who is not directly employed by a qualifying institution, organization, or entity
> identified in section 214(g)(5)(A) or (B) of the Act will qualify for an exemption under such section if
> the H-1B beneficiary will spend at least half of their work time performing job duties at a qualifying
> institution, organization, or entity and those job duties directly further an activity that supports or
> advances one of the fundamental purposes, missions, objectives, or functions of the qualifying
> institution, organization, or entity, namely, either higher education, nonprofit research, or government
> research. Work performed 'at' the qualifying institution may include work performed in the United States
> through telework, remote work, or other off-site work. When considering whether a position is cap-exempt,
> USCIS will focus on the job duties to be performed, rather than where the duties are physically performed.
> (5) If cap-exempt employment ceases, and if the alien is not the beneficiary of a new cap-exempt petition,
> then the alien will be subject to the cap if not previously counted within the 6-year period of authorized
> admission to which the cap-exempt employment applied…"

**Three load-bearing consequences for this profile:**

1. **Paragraph (4) is the "concurrent employment" unlock.** A person who is *not* directly employed by a
   qualifying entity still qualifies **if at least half their work time is spent on job duties at a
   qualifying entity, furthering its research mission**, and **telework/remote/off-site work performed in
   the US counts as work "at" the institution**. In plain terms: a **part-time cap-exempt research
   appointment can anchor the exemption**, and the physical location is not decisive ("USCIS will focus on
   the job duties to be performed, rather than where the duties are physically performed"). This is the
   mechanism by which a cap-exempt role can support concurrent cap-subject employment.
2. **Paragraph (5) is the trap.** If cap-exempt employment stops and the person was never counted against
   the cap, they become **cap-subject**. A cap-exempt anchor is not a permanent immunity; it has to be
   maintained or converted deliberately.
3. **Paragraph (3) routes the definition** to (h)(19)(iii)(C) — the paragraph that actually defines the
   "nonprofit research organization" test.

### 2.4 THE REGULATION SAYS — the "nonprofit research organization" test

Source: 8 CFR 214.2(h)(19)(iii)(C) (LII e-CFR mirror, read 2026-10-14). Verbatim:

> "(C) A nonprofit research organization or government research organization. When a fundamental activity
> of a nonprofit organization is engaging in basic research and/or applied research, that organization is a
> nonprofit research organization. When a fundamental activity of a governmental organization is the
> performance or promotion of basic research and/or applied research, that organization is a government
> research organization. A governmental research organization may be a Federal, State, or local entity. A
> nonprofit research organization or governmental research organization may perform or promote more than
> one fundamental activity. Basic research is general research to gain more comprehensive knowledge or
> understanding of the subject under study, without specific applications in mind. Basic research is also
> research that advances scientific knowledge but does not have specific immediate commercial objectives
> although it may be in fields of present or potential commercial interest. Applied research is research to
> gain knowledge or understanding to determine the means by which a specific, recognized need may be met.
> Applied research includes investigations oriented to discovering new scientific knowledge that has
> specific commercial objectives with respect to products, processes, or services. Both basic research and
> applied research may include research and investigation in the sciences, social sciences, or humanities
> and may include designing, analyzing, and directing the research…"

**The test, stated precisely from that text:**

- The question is whether **"a fundamental activity"** of the organisation **is** engaging in basic and/or
  applied research — **not** whether research is the organisation's *only* activity ("may perform or promote
  more than one fundamental activity"). So a nonprofit that does research *plus* other things can still
  qualify **if research is a fundamental activity**.
- **"Fundamental activity"** is doing real work here: it is a higher bar than "sometimes does research" and
  lower than "research is its sole purpose". This is where the evidence fight lives.
- **Applied research counts**, and it explicitly **may have "specific commercial objectives"**. A nonprofit
  working on commercially-relevant AI/agent software is not disqualified by commercial relevance.
- Both basic and applied research **"may include research and investigation in the sciences, social
  sciences, or humanities"** — so computer science is squarely inside.

> **IN PRACTICE (secondary, weaker).** The common practitioner shorthand is "the organisation's *primary
> purpose* is research". The regulation's operative words are **"a fundamental activity"**, and the
> preamble/definition does not use "primary purpose". `UNVERIFIED`: I did not locate a USCIS policy-manual
> page or AAO precedent decision that adopts the "primary purpose" formulation as the governing test, so
> treat "primary purpose" as **practitioner shorthand, not the regulatory standard**. Counsel should
> brief the actual words.

### 2.5 The degree problem, and the "equivalent" route

**THE REGULATION SAYS — 8 CFR 214.2(h)(4)(iii)(D)(5)** (LII e-CFR mirror, read 2026-10-14), verbatim:

> "For purposes of determining equivalency to a baccalaureate degree in the specialty, three years of
> specialized training and/or work experience must be demonstrated for each year of college-level training
> the alien lacks. For equivalence to an advanced (or Masters) degree, the alien must have a baccalaureate
> degree followed by at least five years of experience in the specialty. If required by a specialty, the
> alien must hold a Doctorate degree or its foreign equivalent."

And 8 CFR 214.2(h)(4)(iii)(D) lists how equivalence may be determined, including **"(3) An evaluation of
education by a reliable credentials evaluation service"** and **"(5) A determination by the Service that
the equivalent of the degree required by the specialty occupation has been acquired through a combination
of education, specialized training, and/or work experience in areas related to the specialty…"**.

**What this means concretely:** for a **cap-exempt H-1B as a specialty occupation**, a person who does not
hold a PhD — or any bachelor's — can still qualify **if** a credentials evaluation (and the employer's
evidence) establishes a baccalaureate-equivalent level, with **three years of specialised training/work
experience substituting for each missing year of college**. For a *master's*-equivalent claim the rule is
stricter: **a baccalaureate degree followed by at least five years of experience in the specialty.**

**Also note:** *"If required by a specialty, the alien must hold a Doctorate degree or its foreign
equivalent."* So the degree question is **specialty-dependent** — the employer's job description and the
occupation's normal minimum requirements drive it, not the beneficiary's CV alone.

## 3. O-1A — the eight criteria

### 3.1 THE REGULATION SAYS — 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)

Source: LII e-CFR mirror of 8 CFR 214.2, read 2026-10-14. The criteria, **verbatim in full**:

> "(iii) Evidentiary criteria for an O-1 alien of extraordinary ability in the fields of science,
> education, business, or athletics. An alien of extraordinary ability in the fields of science, education,
> business, or athletics must demonstrate sustained national or international acclaim and recognition for
> achievements in the field of expertise by providing evidence of:
> (A) Receipt of a major, internationally recognized award, such as the Nobel Prize; or
> (B) At least three of the following forms of documentation:
> (1) Documentation of the alien's receipt of nationally or internationally recognized prizes or awards for
> excellence in the field of endeavor;
> (2) Documentation of the alien's membership in associations in the field for which classification is
> sought, which require outstanding achievements of their members, as judged by recognized national or
> international experts in their disciplines or fields;
> (3) Published material in professional or major trade publications or major media about the alien,
> relating to the alien's work in the field for which classification is sought, which shall include the
> title, date, and author of such published material, and any necessary translation;
> (4) Evidence of the alien's participation on a panel, or individually, as a judge of the work of others
> in the same or in an allied field of specialization to that for which classification is sought;
> (5) Evidence of the alien's original scientific, scholarly, or business-related contributions of major
> significance in the field;
> (6) Evidence of the alien's authorship of scholarly articles in the field, in professional journals, or
> other major media;
> (7) Evidence that the alien has been employed in a critical or essential capacity for organizations and
> establishments that have a distinguished reputation;
> (8) Evidence that the alien has either commanded a high salary or will command a high salary or other
> remuneration for services, evidenced by contracts or other reliable evidence.
> (C) If the criteria in paragraph (o)(3)(iii) of this section do not readily apply to the beneficiary's
> occupation, the petitioner may submit comparable evidence in order to establish the beneficiary's
> eligibility."

### 3.2 Key advantages for this profile — and what is *sourced* vs *asserted*

- **No cap, no lottery.** *Sourced structurally:* INA 214(g)(1)(A) is the H-1B numerical limitation (the
  regulation above calls it "the numerical limitations identified in section 214(g)(1)(A)"), and O-1 is a
  different classification (INA 101(a)(15)(O)). O-1 does not appear among the capped categories in
  INA 214(g). I did **not** find a sentence reading "O-1 has no cap", because caps are imposed by *listing*
  categories — the absence is the point. `UNVERIFIED`: no affirmative source sentence; this is an
  argument from the statute's structure.
- **No degree requirement.** *This is a strong, textually-supported claim:* **the words "degree",
  "baccalaureate", "advanced degree" appear nowhere in the eight O-1A criteria.** Contrast the H-1B
  specialty-occupation definition, which *does* require "attainment of a baccalaureate or higher degree or
  its equivalent as a minimum requirement for entry into the occupation" (8 CFR 214.2(h)(1)(ii)(A)(1)).
  For O-1A the operative standard is the *definitional* one — "one of the small percentage who have arisen
  to the very top of the field of endeavor" (2 USCIS-PM M.4(A)).
- **Premium processing.** USCIS states premium processing gives adjudicative action within **"15 business
  days for most classifications"**
  (<https://www.uscis.gov/forms/all-forms/how-do-i-request-premium-processing>, read 2026-10-14).
- **A **US agent** may petition** — see §3.4. This matters more than the cap-exemption for someone with no
  employer.

### 3.3 IN PRACTICE — O-1A is often stronger than cap-subject H-1B for this profile

Secondary/experiential, and flagged as such: for an independent researcher with a publication and citation
record but no PhD and no employer lined up, O-1A is frequently the better instrument than a cap-subject
H-1B because it (a) removes the lottery, (b) has no degree gate, and (c) allows an agent petitioner. The
counterweight is that O-1A is a **facts-and-circumstances "top of field"** judgment with a **consultation**
requirement, and a cap-exempt H-1B is more mechanical *once a qualifying employer exists*. These are
different risks: O-1A risks a discretionary denial; H-1B risks never getting a number.

### 3.4 The agent-as-petitioner question and self-petition limits

**THE POLICY MANUAL SAYS — 2 USCIS-PM M.3(A)** (<https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-2-part-m-chapter-3>,
read 2026-10-14), verbatim:

> "A U.S. employer may file an O-1 or O-2 Petition for a Nonimmigrant Worker (Form I-129). A U.S. agent may
> also file such a petition when it involves workers who are traditionally self-employed or workers who use
> agents to arrange short-term employment on their behalf with numerous employers, and in cases where a
> foreign employer authorizes the agent to act in its behalf. A U.S. agent may be: The actual employer of
> the beneficiary; The representative of both the employer and the beneficiary; or A person or entity
> authorized by the employer to act for, or in place of, the employer as its agent.
> **O beneficiaries may not petition for themselves.** However, a separate legal entity owned by the
> beneficiary, such as a corporation or limited liability company, may file the petition on their behalf."

**This is decisive and precise:**
- **Self-petition is barred** for O-1: *"O beneficiaries may not petition for themselves."*
- **An agent may petition** for "workers who are traditionally self-employed". That is the practical
  workaround for an independent researcher.
- **A separate legal entity owned by the beneficiary (corporation or LLC) may file.** So incorporating is a
  real structural option — though whether that entity is a genuine "agent"/employer for O-1 purposes, and
  what it must show (contract, wage terms), is exactly the kind of question to put to counsel. The
  regulation's agent provisions require the agent "performing the function of an employer" to provide the
  **contractual agreement between the agent and the beneficiary which specifies the wage offered and the
  other terms and conditions of employment** (8 CFR 214.2(o), agent-as-petitioner provisions, read
  2026-10-14).

**Consultation / advisory opinions.** INA 214(c)(6)(A)(i) requires a **"written advisory opinion from a peer
group (or other person or persons of its choosing, which may include a labor organization) with expertise in
the specific field involved"**, and the statute states **"The Attorney General shall give such weight to
advisory opinions provided under this section as the Attorney General determines, in his sole discretion, to
be appropriate."** (govinfo, 8 U.S.C. 1184(c)(6), read 2026-10-14). The regulation defines **"Peer group"**
as **"a group or organization which is comprised of practitioners of the alien's occupation"** (8 CFR
214.2(o)(3)(ii)).

**Cost.** `UNVERIFIED` in amount — see §13. The *structure* is: Form I-129 filing fee for the O
classification + optional Form I-907 premium processing fee + any attorney and advisory-op costs. Do not
budget from this memo; check the USCIS fee schedule at filing.

### 4.2 EB-2 NIW — the *Matter of Dhanasar* three-prong test

**THE POLICY MANUAL SAYS — 6 USCIS-PM F.5(D)(2)** (<https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-6-part-f-chapter-5>,
read 2026-10-14), verbatim:

> "USCIS may grant a national interest waiver as a matter of discretion if the petitioner demonstrates
> eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence, based on the following three prongs:
> - The alien's proposed endeavor has both substantial merit and national importance;
> - The alien is well positioned to advance the proposed endeavor; and
> - On balance, it would be beneficial to the United States to waive the job offer and thus the permanent
>   labor certification requirements."

**The decision and its citation are verified.** From the footnotes on that same USCIS page, verbatim:
*"See Matter of Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec. 884, 886 n.3 (AAO 2016)."* and *"See Matter of Dhanasar, 26 I&N Dec.
884, 893 (AAO 2016)."* The page also gives a worked example, verbatim: *"in Matter of Dhanasar, the
occupation was engineer while the endeavor was engaging in research and development relating to air and
space propulsion systems."* The USCIS footnote links to the decision file at
<https://www.justice.gov/eoir/page/file/920996/download>. **Caveat:** that URL served me an
`application/pdf` that my fetcher could not decode, so **the prong wording above is quoted from the USCIS
Policy Manual, not from the decision text itself.** The citation (26 I&N Dec. 884 (AAO 2016)) and the
DOJ URL are verified; the decision's own wording is `UNVERIFIED` in this memo.

**NIW is self-petitionable and waives PERM.** 6 USCIS-PM F.5(D), verbatim: *"A petition filed with a request
for a national interest waiver on behalf of a person does not need to be supported by a job offer; therefore,
the person may file as a self-petitioner. A waiver of a job offer also includes a waiver of the permanent
labor certification requirement."*

**The NIW's own gate — this is the catch for this profile.** 6 USCIS-PM F.5(D)(1), verbatim: *"To establish
eligibility for a national interest waiver, a petitioner must first demonstrate the person's qualification
for the underlying EB-2 visa classification as either a member of the professions holding an advanced degree
or an individual of exceptional ability in the sciences, arts, or business. If the person does not have the
qualifications for the EB-2 classification, the petition is statutorily ineligible for the national interest
waiver."*

**And there is an explicit escape hatch that reads as if written for this profile.** 6 USCIS-PM F.5(D)(1),
verbatim: *"For example, a person who has computer science education or experience that does not equate to
the definition of an advanced degree and who is proposing an endeavor related to computer science may
qualify as a person with exceptional ability if USCIS determines that they have satisfied at least three of
the six regulatory criteria and demonstrated their exceptional ability in an area such as computer science,
which is directly related to their endeavor."*

So: **a no-PhD AI researcher is not shut out of NIW — they must route through "exceptional ability"**, not
"advanced degree". The six exceptional-ability criteria (6 USCIS-PM F.5(B)(2)) include *"Evidence of
recognition for achievements and significant contributions to the industry or field by peers, governmental
entities, or professional or business organizations"* — and the Manual notes the advanced-degree route also
permits **"a U.S. bachelor's degree, or a foreign degree evaluated to be the equivalent of a U.S. bachelor's
degree, plus 5 years of progressive, post-degree work experience"**, while **"A beneficiary who does not
possess at least a U.S. bachelor's degree or a foreign equivalent degree is ineligible for this
classification"** (6 USCIS-PM F.5(A)(2)).

### 5.1 J-1 research scholar — and the INA 212(e) two-year trap

**THE STATUTE SAYS — INA 212(e), 8 U.S.C. 1182(e)** (govinfo, *United States Code, 2023 Edition*, Title 8,

> "…the Attorney General may waive the requirement of such two-year foreign residence abroad in the case of

> 1184(l) of this title: And provided further, That, except in the case of an alien described in clause

**persecution** claim, or a **state public health department** request (the fragment above confirms these
channels exist). **But if it is not waived, the person cannot change status to H or L or adjust to
permanent residence until they have spent two years abroad.** The Skills List is country-specific — another
`NATIONALITY-UNKNOWN` dependency. `UNVERIFIED`: the current Skills List contents for the subject's
nationality, and the specific regulation implementing 212(e) (22 CFR 62.20 / 8 CFR 212.7(c)), which I did

## 8. Recommended sequencing — student route just closed

Presented as a **draft plan for counsel to review**, not as advice. The ordering principle is: **get
independent, non-student status first; build the permanent record in parallel; never let the cap-exempt
anchor lapse silently.**

1. **Immediately — establish what status, if any, is held right now, and for how long.** This is the first
   question for counsel, and it is the only item on this list that is urgent. A school's decision to stop
   issuing OPT/CPT recommendations is a *school* decision; whether it changes the person's existing status,
   and what the grace period is, are legal questions. Do not let this memo substitute for that call.
2. **In parallel — start the evidence file (§7) now.** It is route-independent and it is the long pole:
   citation snapshots, completed-review proof, letters, and open-source impact take weeks to assemble
   properly. Nothing on the route list can be filed without it.
3. **Route priority for a person with US presence and no employer — O-1A, with an agent petitioner.**
   Reasons, in order of strength: **no cap and no lottery** (removes the deadline that just closed),
   **no degree requirement in the criteria**, **agent petitioning available** including for the
   traditionally self-employed, and **premium processing in 15 business days**. The structural fix that must
   exist first is the **agent or owned-entity petitioner** and the **advisory opinion**.
4. **In parallel, prepare EB-1A as the permanent track.** It is **self-petitionable with no job offer and no
   labor certification** (8 CFR 204.5(h)(3)(5)), it has **no degree requirement**, and it is the route whose
   priority date is worth holding early. If §4.3's backlog premise holds once the bulletin is checked, EB-1A
   is the better permanent queue than EB-2 NIW; if it does not, EB-2 NIW remains worthwhile as a second,
   self-petitioned I-140.
5. **Hold EB-2 NIW as the fallback permanent track**, routed through **"exceptional ability"** rather than
   "advanced degree" if no bachelor's-equivalent exists — 6 USCIS-PM F.5(D)(1) expressly contemplates a
   computer-science person whose education "does not equate to the definition of an advanced degree".
6. **Pursue a cap-exempt H-1B concurrently, as the steady-state work authorisation.** A part-time research
   affiliation at a qualifying entity, documented as **≥50% of work time** furthering that entity's research
   mission, supports the exemption under 8 CFR 214.2(h)(8)(iii)(F)(4) — and **the duties, not the physical
   location, control**. This is the route that most directly replaces OPT as a *work-authorisation* answer,
   so it should not wait on the O-1A.
7. **Do not let the anchor lapse.** Under 8 CFR 214.2(h)(8)(iii)(F)(5), if cap-exempt employment ceases and
   the person was never counted against the cap, they become cap-subject. Any transition between a
   cap-exempt role and a cap-subject one must be planned, not discovered.
8. **Nationality-dependent routes (H-1B1 / TN / E-3 / the USMCA B-1 research branch) are on hold** until
   nationality is established. If nationality turns out to be Chile, Singapore, Canada, Mexico, or
   Australia, one of them may be materially cheaper and faster than anything above — so establish
   nationality **now** and re-open this memo's §5 if it matches.
9. **J-1 research scholar and B-1 are stopgaps, not solutions** — J-1 because of the INA 212(e) two-year trap
   (where the J-1 was government-financed or the nationality's Skills List applies), B-1 because it is not
   work authorisation and bars enrolment in a course of study.
